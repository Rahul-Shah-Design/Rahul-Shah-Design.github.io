### Throughline

*What happened inside that jar between 2 pm and 11 pm?*

A full answer contains, claim by claim:

- T1 — Two different living populations share the jar and eat the same sugar. **(M1)**
- T2 — The flour is not food when it goes in; enzymes in the flour cut it into sugar, so the
  sugar supply per feed is made on site and is finite. **(M1)**
- T3 — The yeast's waste is gas; the rise is gas trapped in a protein web, not new material. **(M1)**
- T4 — The bacteria's waste is acid, and acid has no way out of the jar. **(M1)**
- T5 — The rise is a population doubling toward a ceiling set by that finite sugar. **(M2)**
- T6 — The peak is the moment the sugar runs short, not a finish line. **(M2)**
- T7 — The fall is two failures at once: gas production slowing *and* the protein web being
  softened by the accumulated acid so it stops holding what is still being made. **(M2)**
- T8 — Temperature multiplies the speed of all of it, so a clock-based feeding schedule is a
  bet on the room. **(M2)**
- T9 — The acid ratchets up across days, and below about pH 3.5 the yeast stalls while the
  bacteria carry on — the culture goes sour, sluggish, and thin. **(M3)**
- T10 — Feeding is mostly *dilution*: the feed ratio decides what fraction of yesterday's acid
  survives into today's jar. **(M3)**
- T11 — Starvation, cold and acid **stall**; heat above about 40 °C, drying out, and mould
  **kill**. Hooch is a stall signal, not a death certificate. **(M3)**
- T12 — Therefore: their two starters were almost certainly never dead, and the third one
  lives on a bigger feed at a stable temperature. **(M3)**

This needs the whole course. One paragraph could say "it over-fermented," which is a label,
not an answer, and would leave them making the same feed.

The throughline goes *further into the mechanism they asked about* — rise and death. It does
not slide sideways into bread: no bulk fermentation, no shaping, no scoring, no crumb. Those
are a different course and they are the tempting detour here.

### Modules

**Module 1 — "The crowd in the jar."** What is alive in there, what it eats, and what the two
tenants leave behind. Four sections.

- M1-C1 — The jar holds two different kinds of living thing: wild yeast (a single-celled
  fungus, the same family as bread yeast) and lactic acid bacteria (the same group that turns
  milk into yogurt). Roughly ten to a hundred bacteria for every yeast cell.
- M1-C2 — Flour is not food yet. Starch is a long chain of sugar units locked end to end.
  Flour carries its own enzymes — proteins that cut things apart — that snip the chain into
  small sugars. Whole wheat carries more of those enzymes and more microbes on the bran, which
  is why a spoon of it wakes a jar up.
- M1-C3 — Yeast waste is carbon dioxide gas plus a little alcohol. The gas is caught in
  gluten, the stretchy web wheat protein forms when flour meets water. The rise is trapped
  gas. The jar does not gain material; on a scale it loses a little.
- M1-C4 — Bacteria waste is acid: lactic acid, the mild yogurt one, and acetic acid, the sharp
  vinegar one. The gas leaves through the lid. The acid stays.
- One sentence on the trade, not more: the bacteria eat a sugar the yeast largely cannot, and
  leak out one it can, which is why these two coexist instead of one crowding the other out.

**Module 2 — "The rise, the peak, and the long slide down."** The shape of one feed, in time.
Four sections. Arc: mechanism → the leap → the mess → the reveal.

- M2-C1 — The rise is a crowd doubling toward a ceiling, not a pile getting bigger. Doubling
  time, not amount added, sets the pace. *(rests on M1-C1, M1-C3)*
- M2-C2 — The peak is the moment the sugar runs short. It is a shortage, not a finish line,
  and where it lands is set almost entirely by how much old starter you kept back. *(rests on
  M1-C2, M2-C1)*
- M2-C3 — The fall is two failures at the same time: gas made per hour drops as sugar runs
  out, and the accumulated acid softens the gluten web so a shrinking fraction of the gas is
  held. Gas is still being produced at hour twelve. *(rests on M1-C3, M1-C4, M2-C2)*
- M2-C4 — Every rate here roughly doubles for each 10 °C the jar warms. A feeding schedule set
  by the clock is a bet on the room temperature. *(rests on M2-C1, M2-C2, M2-C3)*

**Module 3 — "Why it dies, and how the third one lives."** Five sections.

- M3-C1 — Acid accumulates and never leaves. A fresh mix sits near pH 6; a ripe jar near pH 4;
  a long-overdue one near pH 3.5. pH is the sourness number: lower is more sour. *(rests on M1-C4)*
- M3-C2 — Below about pH 3.5 the yeast slows hard while the bacteria keep going, so the
  population tilts. The result is a jar that smells strong and lifts nothing. *(rests on M3-C1,
  M2-C3)*
- M3-C3 — Feeding is mostly dilution. A 1:1:1 feed leaves a third of yesterday's acid in the
  jar; a 1:5:5 feed leaves one part in eleven. Under-feeding ratchets the acid up day over day.
  *(rests on M3-C1, M2-C2)* — **this is where their half-cup-and-half-cup routine gets named.**
- M3-C4 — What actually kills, and what only stalls. Kills: above roughly 40 °C (104 °F, and
  hot tap water is hotter than that), drying to a crust, mould with fuzz or colour. Stalls:
  cold, starvation, acid. Hooch is a stall signal. *(rests on M3-C2)*
- M3-C5 — The protocol, written for their kitchen and their measuring cups.

## MODULE 2

# The rise, the peak, and the long slide down

Module 1 put two tenants in your jar and gave each one a waste product: gas from the yeast,
acid from the bacteria. That is a snapshot. It does not yet explain why a jar that looked
perfect at two in the afternoon looked finished at eleven at night.

This module is that stretch of time. It goes in four moves. First, what the rise actually is —
and it is not the jar getting bigger. Second, what the peak is, which is not what it looks
like. Third, why the fall has **two** causes running at the same time, which is the piece you
told me you were stuck on. Fourth, the one thing on your counter that multiplies all of it,
and why it means "feed it once a day" is not really a schedule.

---

## 1. The rise is a crowd doubling, not a pile getting bigger

Feed the jar at eight. Look at nine and there are maybe a dozen bubbles clinging to the glass
near the bottom. Look at ten and there are two or three dozen. At eleven, closer to a hundred.
At noon the whole side of the jar is webbed with them and you have stopped being able to count.

Nothing is being poured in. What is happening is that each yeast cell in there splits into two
cells, and then each of those splits into two. Every cell that gets made is a new cell that can
make more cells. So the crowd does not grow by adding a fixed amount each hour — it grows by
multiplying.

That is why the bubbles seem to come out of nowhere. For the first couple of hours, doubling a
small number still gives you a small number, and the jar looks like it is doing nothing. The
same doubling, four hours later, is being applied to a much bigger number, and now it looks
dramatic. The rate never changed. Only the number it was acting on.

The time it takes the crowd to double is the **doubling time**, and for the yeast in a
counter-top starter it is roughly two and a half hours in a warm kitchen. Hold on to that
number; section 4 is entirely about what changes it.

One thing this does *not* mean. The jar is not making more jar. Module 1's point stands: the
rise is carbon dioxide gas caught in the gluten web, and gas weighs almost nothing. The crowd
doubling is what produces the gas. The gas is what you see.

### Figure 1 — one feed, from eight in the morning

**What it plots.** Two lines against the same horizontal axis, hours since feeding, running
from 0 to 18.

- The **crowd line** (thin, grey): the number of cells in the jar, as a percentage of the most
  the jar could ever hold on this one feed. It starts at 33%, rises in a stretched S — slow,
  then steep through the middle hours, then flattening — and is at 93% by hour twelve.
- The **height line** (thick, and the one to watch): how tall the paste stands, as a multiple
  of where it started. It starts at 1.00×, climbs to a rounded top of **2.20× at hour four**,
  and then comes down — 1.94× at hour six, 1.57× at hour eight, 1.24× at hour twelve, and
  still drifting down at 1.09× by hour eighteen.

The two lines do not have the same shape, and that mismatch is the whole rest of this module.

**Where these numbers came from.** I ran a small model, not your jar. It has three rules: the
crowd doubles on a fixed clock until the food runs short; the gas produced tracks how much
food has been eaten; and the fraction of that gas the jar can still hold falls as acid builds
up. One number in it was tuned — the doubling time — so that a jar fed one part old starter to
one part flour to one part water at 78 °F peaks at four hours, which is what people who watch
their jars with a rubber band on the glass actually measure. Everything else on the chart
follows from that. **Where it breaks:** it assumes the temperature holds steady all day, and
yours does not. Section 4 fixes that and re-runs it on your real counter.

**What to notice.** At hour twelve the crowd is at 93% of its ceiling — nearly the most cells
this jar will ever hold — and the height is back down to 1.24×. The jar is at its fullest of
living things at the moment it looks emptiest.

## 2. The peak is the moment the sugar runs short

Two jars on the same warm counter. Into jar A you stir one tablespoon of ripe starter with a
cup of fresh flour and water. Into jar B you stir four tablespoons into the same amount of
fresh flour and water. Jar B domes first — well before jar A — and jar B comes back down
first too.

Nothing about jar B is stronger. It just started closer to the finish. Module 1 said the flour
carries a fixed amount of sugar that its own enzymes can release for one feed. That fixed
amount sets a ceiling on how many cells the jar can support. Jar B started with four times the
crowd, so it had far fewer doublings left to do before it hit that ceiling.

So the peak is not the jar announcing it is ready. **The peak is the sugar running short.** It
is the moment the crowd stops being able to grow because there is not enough left to grow on,
and the gas that had been arriving fast starts arriving slower.

Which means the thing that decides *when* your jar peaks is mostly one decision you make with
a spoon: how much of yesterday's jar you keep back before you add the fresh flour.

That decision has a shorthand. **1 : 5 : 5** means one part old starter, five parts fresh
flour, five parts water — by weight. Written that way, your routine is **1 : 0.5 : 0.5**,
because half a cup of wet starter weighs about twice what half a cup of dry flour weighs. It
is the smallest feed there is. Set that aside for now; the instrument is about to show you what
it does.

### Instrument — the peak clock

**Control:** a ratio dial with six positions — 1:0.5:0.5, 1:1:1, 1:2:2, 1:3:3, 1:5:5,
1:10:10. Temperature is pinned at 78 °F for now.

**What the control changes:** the size of the starting crowd as a fraction of the ceiling, and
therefore how many doublings the jar has left to do before it runs out of sugar.

**What visibly changes:** the height curve from Figure 1 slides bodily to the right as you feed
bigger, and a marker slides along the hours axis to sit on the top of the curve. Two readouts
change with it — **doublings needed** and **peak at ___ hours**.

**Guided experiment.**

1. Set the dial to **1:1:1**. Read the two readouts: *doublings needed 1.6*, *peak at 4.1
   hours*. That is the curve you already saw in Figure 1.
2. Now drag the dial all the way to **1:10:10**. The readouts move to *doublings needed 4.4*
   and *peak at 7.4 hours*. Watch the curve while you drag: it does not get taller and it does
   not change shape. It only stretches out along the time axis.
3. Now drag the other way, to **1:0.5:0.5** — your routine. *Doublings needed 1.0*, *peak at
   3.5 hours*.

**What to notice.** Going from 1:1:1 to 1:10:10 is ten times the flour, but only about three
extra doublings, and less than double the time to peak. Doublings are cheap to buy. And at
your setting, a jar fed at eight in the morning has peaked before lunch.

**Check — set the control.** Leave the temperature at 78 °F. Move the ratio dial until the
peak marker lands past seven hours. Which is the smallest feed that gets you there?

- **1:1:1** — *Peak marker reads 4.1 hours. This gets you barely past the halfway point. One
  part flour to one part starter only buys you about 1.6 doublings, and 1.6 doublings at two
  and a half hours each is four hours, not seven.*
- **1:2:2** — *4.9 hours. Closer, but adding one more part of flour has bought you less than an
  hour. Each extra doubling costs the same time, and doublings go up slowly as flour goes up
  fast.*
- **1:5:5** — *6.2 hours. This is the one people expect to be enough, and it is not — you are
  still most of an hour short. Watch the marker: it is sitting just left of the seven.*
- **1:10:10** — **Correct.** *7.4 hours. This is the smallest position on the dial that pushes
  the marker past seven. Note how much flour it took to buy those three extra hours over a 1:1:1 feed
  — this is the reason section 4 exists, because there is a cheaper lever than flour.*

### ✓ Check before section 3

Two jars, same flour, same water, same warm spot on the counter. Into jar A you stir **one**
tablespoon of ripe starter with a cup of fresh flour and water. Into jar B you stir **four**
tablespoons with the same cup of fresh flour and water. Which one domes first?

*(Multiple choice, one answer.)*

- **Jar A** — *This is the answer you get if you read "more starter" as "more food to get
  through." It is the other way round. The food is the fresh flour, and both jars got the same
  amount of it. What jar A has is a smaller starting crowd, so it has more doubling to do
  before it reaches the ceiling — which makes it later, not earlier.*
- **Jar B** — **Correct.** *Jar B starts with four times the crowd, so it needs two fewer
  doublings to reach the same ceiling, and at roughly two and a half hours a doubling that is
  about five hours saved. Same flour, same ceiling, earlier arrival.*
- **They dome at the same time; the flour is the same** — *Right that the flour is the same,
  and that is what fixes the ceiling — so both jars top out at about the same height. But
  height and timing are separate. The crowd each jar starts with decides how long it takes to
  get there.*
- **You cannot tell without knowing the flour** — *The flour is worth asking about, and it does
  set the ceiling: how much sugar there is to release, which caps how many cells the jar can
  hold. But it is the same flour in both jars here, so it cancels out and the starting crowd is
  the only thing left that differs.*

## 3. The fall is two things going wrong at the same time

Here is the fact that does not fit the story you have.

At hour twelve your jar looks finished — caved in, slack, wet. At hour twelve the yeast is
still making gas. Not a trickle: about **a third of the rate it was making it at the peak**,
hour after hour, and it will still be making some at hour eighteen.

So the fall cannot be "the yeast died." Something else is happening, and it is happening
alongside the slowdown, not instead of it.

Two things fall at once:

**One — gas made per hour drops.** The sugar is running short, so the crowd stops growing, so
the total rate of gas production comes off its top speed. This is gradual. It is a slope, not
a cliff.

**Two — the fraction of that gas the jar can still hold falls off a cliff.** This is the one
you have never had a name for, and it is most of the collapse.

Why the web lets go: the bacteria have been making acid this whole time, and module 1 said the
acid has nowhere to leave. It builds. Acid does two things to gluten, the stretchy protein web
holding every bubble. It weakens the web directly — the strands stop clinging to each other as
well. And it switches on **proteases**, which are enzymes in the flour whose job is cutting
protein into pieces. Proteases are half asleep in a fresh, barely-sour jar and wide awake in a
sour one. They spend the afternoon cutting up the very web that is holding your bubbles.

So the bubbles merge, break, and vent out through the top. The height falls even while gas
keeps arriving.

### The one image for this

You have whipped egg whites and left the bowl on the counter for half an hour. It stands up
stiff, and then it slumps, and there is a puddle of thin clear liquid underneath it.

Map it onto the jar:

- the bubbles you beat in → the carbon dioxide the yeast makes
- the protein film that sets around each bubble → the gluten web
- the foam standing tall → your risen jar
- the puddle of thin liquid that weeps out underneath → **the grey layer on top of your jar**

The grey liquid you have been calling hooch is that. Water, plus the alcohol the yeast makes
alongside its gas, draining out of a web that has stopped holding onto them. It is not the jar
asking for food. It is the jar telling you that it went past its peak a long time ago.

**Where the image breaks, and it matters:** nobody is adding new bubbles to your bowl of egg
whites. In your jar, gas keeps arriving the entire time. That is exactly why a starter can sit
there looking stable and domed for an hour while it is already failing — new gas is coming in
at nearly the same rate old gas is escaping, so the height barely moves, right up until it
tips.

And the smell. The yeast has been making alcohol all afternoon. Alcohol plus the sharp vinegary
acid from the bacteria makes a compound called ethyl acetate, which is the solvent in most
non-acetone nail polish removers. When your eleven-o'clock jar smelled like nail polish
remover, it was not a figure of speech. It was the same chemical.

## 4. Temperature is the speed knob on all of it

Your jar sits on the counter next to the stove. At eight in the morning that counter is about
62 °F. By one in the afternoon, with a burner going, it is up near 76 °F. Same jar. Same feed.

Every rate in that jar — how fast the crowd doubles, how fast gas gets made, how fast the acid
piles up, how fast the proteases chew the web — moves roughly together with temperature. The
rule of thumb worth memorising: **warm it by 10 °C, about 18 °F, and everything roughly
doubles in speed.** Cool it by the same and everything roughly halves.

Which is why "feed it once a day" is not a schedule. It is a bet on your kitchen. In a 62 °F
room a once-a-day feed might be roughly right. In a 78 °F room the same jar has been past its
peak for most of a day by the time you get to it.

Cold does not weaken a starter. It postpones it. Heat is the one that can genuinely end it, and
only above about 40 °C — 104 °F — where the yeast starts dying rather than slowing.

### Instrument — the counter thermostat

**Control:** a temperature dial, 50 °F to 110 °F. The ratio dial from section 2 stays on the
screen and stays wherever you leave it.

**What the control changes:** the doubling time of the crowd, and with it every other rate in
the jar, by the ten-degree rule.

**What visibly changes:** the whole height curve stretches out as you go colder and squeezes up
as you go warmer, with the peak marker riding along the top of it. Two
readouts move: **doubling time ___ hours** and **peak at ___ hours**. Above 104 °F a band
across the top of the chart turns red, the curve stops climbing wherever it had got to, and
stays flat.

**Guided experiment.**

1. Set the ratio to **1:1:1** and the thermostat to **78 °F**. Readouts: *doubling time 2.6
   hours*, *peak at 4.1 hours*.
2. Drag the thermostat down to **62 °F** — your counter first thing in the morning. Readouts:
   *doubling time 4.8 hours*, *peak at 7.6 hours*. Now look at the top of the curve: the peak
   height readout still says **2.2×**. It did not move.
3. Drag up past **104 °F** into the red band and watch the curve go flat and stay flat, whatever
   you do to the ratio dial afterwards. Then go and run your kitchen tap hot and put a
   thermometer in it — most kitchen taps deliver 120 to 140 °F.

**What to notice.** Sixteen degrees Fahrenheit nearly doubled the time to peak and changed the
peak height by nothing at all. A cold jar is not a weak jar. It is the same jar, later. The red
band is the only place on this dial where anything actually stops being alive.

**Check — numeric, within 1.5 hours.** Set the ratio dial to **1:5:5** and the thermostat to
**62 °F**. How many hours until the peak marker?

- **Correct answer: 11.5 hours** (anything from 10 to 13 counts).
- *If you answered around 6:* that is 1:5:5 at 78 °F. You changed the ratio and left the
  thermostat warm. Going from 78 °F to 62 °F is 16 degrees down, which roughly doubles every
  time in the jar.
- *If you answered around 7.5:* that is 1:1:1 at 62 °F. You moved the thermostat but left the
  ratio where it was. Both dials are in play in this one.
- *If you answered around 3.5:* that is your own 1:0.5:0.5 routine at 78 °F — the setting the
  instrument was left on at the end of section 2. Reset both dials and read again.
- *If you answered over 20:* the ten-degree rule doubles per 10 °C, which is 18 °F, not per
  degree. Sixteen degrees Fahrenheit is a little under one doubling, so a bit less than 2×
  slower — not 5× or 10× slower.

## End of module — five questions

**1.** You feed exactly as you always do, and the jar peaks in three hours instead of the usual
six. What is the most likely cause?

*(Multiple choice, one answer.)*

- **You kept back more old starter, relative to the fresh flour, than you usually do** —
  **Correct.** *A bigger starting crowd needs fewer doublings to reach the same ceiling. Halving
  the doublings roughly halves the time to peak, which is what the peak clock does when you drag
  it from 1:2:2 to 1:0.5:0.5.*
- **The starter has matured and got stronger** — *A faster peak feels like strength, and it is
  the read most people reach for. But nothing on the peak clock corresponds to "strength." What
  moved the marker was the size of the crowd you started with and the temperature of the room.
  A jar that peaks in three hours is a jar with less runway, not a better jar.*
- **Your flour was old and weaker** — *Old flour would matter, and it would matter in the
  direction of the ceiling: less enzyme activity, sugar released more slowly, so the jar takes
  **longer**, not shorter. Wrong direction.*
- **The kitchen was colder than usual** — *Cold slows every rate in the jar, so a colder kitchen
  pushes the peak later. On the thermostat, dragging from 78 °F to 62 °F moved the peak from 4.1
  hours to 7.6. You would need a warmer kitchen, not a colder one.*

**2.** Your counter holds at 62 °F all day — a cold week, no cooking. You feed one part starter
to five parts flour to five parts water at eight in the morning. About what time does it peak?

*(Numeric entry, in hours after feeding. Correct within ±1.5 hours.)*

- **Correct answer: 11.5 hours** — *about half past seven in the evening. Accepted: 10 to 13.*
- *Around 6:* that is the right ratio at the wrong temperature — 78 °F rather than 62 °F.
- *Around 7.5:* that is 62 °F with a 1:1:1 feed. The bigger feed adds about two extra doublings
  and each one is slower in a cold room, which is why the two effects compound.
- *Around 4:* that is a 1:1:1 feed in a warm room — neither dial in the position the question
  set.

**3.** Put these in the order they happen, from the moment water hits the flour to the moment
the surface of the paste lifts.

*(Drag to reorder. Six steps, presented shuffled.)*

1. Water soaks into the flour.
2. Enzymes already present in the flour cut starch chains into small sugars.
3. A yeast cell takes in one of those sugars.
4. The yeast cell releases carbon dioxide.
5. The gas collects into a pocket held by the gluten web.
6. The surface of the paste lifts.

- **Correct order: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6.**
- *If you put "a yeast cell takes in sugar" before "enzymes cut starch into sugars":* this is
  the most common way to get this wrong, and it comes from treating flour as food the moment it
  goes in. It is not. Starch is a long chain of sugar units locked end to end, and no tenant in
  the jar can take that in whole. Something has to cut it first, and that something is the
  flour's own enzymes.
- *If you put "the gas collects into a pocket" before "the yeast releases carbon dioxide":* the
  gas has to exist before it can be trapped. The pocket is not there waiting — it is opened by
  gas pushing the web apart.
- *If you put "the surface lifts" before "gas collects into a pocket":* the surface lifting is
  the last thing in the chain, and it is the only step in the list you can see from outside the
  jar. Everything above it is why.

**4.** Borrow a kitchen scale for five minutes. You put the jar on it with the lid off, right
after feeding: **480 g**. Six hours later it has risen to a bit over twice its height. You
weigh it again, still no lid. What does the scale say?

*(Multiple choice, one answer.)*

- **About 960 g** — *This is the answer if rising means the jar is making more material. It is
  not. Doubling the height is doubling the volume the same stuff occupies, because it is now
  full of gas pockets. The gas has almost no weight, and none of it came from outside the jar.*
- **About 480 g, maybe a gram or two under** — **Correct.** *Nothing was added and nothing much
  left. What did leave is a small amount of carbon dioxide that escaped past the rim instead of
  staying trapped, and a trace of alcohol evaporating — a gram or two over six hours. The jar
  is the same weight and twice the size.*
- **About 240 g** — *This is the answer if the tenants "use up" the flour and it disappears. They
  do rearrange it — starch into sugar, sugar into gas and alcohol and acid — but rearranging
  does not destroy it. Only the part that leaves as gas is gone, and that is grams.*
- **It depends on how much whole wheat is in the mix** — *Whole wheat changes how fast the sugar
  gets released, so it changes when the jar peaks. It does not change the fact that nothing is
  entering or leaving the jar except a little gas.*

**5.** Four points are marked on the gas-made and gas-held curves from Figure 2: **A** at hour
two, **B** at hour four, **C** at hour seven, **D** at hour thirteen. Drag each description onto
its point.

*(Drag to label. Four labels, four points.)*

- **"Gas arriving fast, web still holding nearly all of it — over 95%"** → **A (hour two).**
  *Production is close to its maximum and almost nothing has escaped yet. This is the part of
  the afternoon that looks like the whole story if you only ever check the jar once.*
- **"Gas arriving at its fastest, web just starting to let go — around three-quarters held"** →
  **B (hour four).** *This is the top of the height curve. Notice what it is not: it is not the
  moment production stops. Production is at its peak here too. The height turns over because
  the held fraction has just started falling faster than production is still climbing.*
- **"Gas still arriving at about three-quarters of top speed, web holding under a third"** →
  **C (hour seven).** *The two lines have come apart completely. The yeast has barely slowed and
  the jar has lost most of what it made. If you were reading the jar by its height here you
  would conclude the yeast had failed, and you would be wrong by a factor of three.*
- **"Gas nearly finished, web holding almost none"** → **D (hour thirteen).** *Now both have
  gone. Production is down to about a quarter of peak and the web is holding 5%. This is your
  eleven-o'clock jar.*

- *If you put A and B the wrong way round:* both have gas arriving at nearly full speed, so
  production cannot separate them. The held fraction can — 98% at hour two against 77% at hour
  four.
- *If you put C at hour thirteen:* check the production number in the label. Three-quarters of
  top speed is a jar still working hard. By hour thirteen production has dropped to about a quarter of it.
