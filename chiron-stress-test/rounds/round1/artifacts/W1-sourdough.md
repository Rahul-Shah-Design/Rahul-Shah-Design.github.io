## HOOK

You feed the jar at eight in the morning. Some of the old stuff goes in the bin, fresh flour
and water go in, you stir it, and you set the lid on loose.

At two in the afternoon it looks perfect. Domed on top, more than doubled, a web of bubbles
down the side of the glass, and it smells like plain yogurt.

Then you get busy. You come back at eleven at night. The dome is gone. The surface has caved
in and gone slack and wet, there is a thin layer of greyish liquid sitting on top, and the
smell has turned sharp — closer to nail polish remover than to yogurt.

Nothing was added. Nothing was taken out. Same jar, nine hours later.

What do you think happened inside that jar between two in the afternoon and eleven at night?

### Learner

Home cook. No science past high school. Two dead starters. Wants the third to live.
Vocabulary they actually used: *hooch, feed, doubled, gave up, dumped in, measuring cup.*
No scale. Volume measuring. King Arthur AP flour, occasional whole wheat. Jar on the counter
beside the stove: about 62 °F in the morning, up near 76 °F when they cook.

Read from their first attempt: they have the right instinct (food runs out) and three things
tangled with it — (1) "ran out of food" and "died" are the same event to them, (2) hooch is a
symptom they can name but not explain, (3) they have no separation between the two organisms
in the jar. They did not use the word *bacteria*. They did not mention acid, temperature, or
their feed ratio. Their feeding routine is, by weight, roughly **1 part starter : 0.5 parts
flour : 0.5 parts water** — a half-cup of starter weighs about twice what a half-cup of flour
weighs — which is the smallest feed on the dial and almost certainly the reason both previous
starters died. That diagnosis is the course's payload, and it cannot be handed over in module
1 because it needs the population clock and the acid ratchet first.

Everything gets built in their world: their jar, their flour, their counter next to the stove,
their measuring cups, their 8 am feed.

### Throughline

*What happened inside that jar between 2 pm and 11 pm?*

A full answer contains, claim by claim:

- T1 — Two different living populations share the jar and eat the same sugar. **(M1)**
- T2 — The flour is not food when it goes in; enzymes in the flour cut it into sugar, so the
  sugar supply per feed is made on site and is finite. **(M1)**
- T3 — The yeast's waste is gas; the rise is gas trapped in a protein web, not new material. **(M1)**
- T4 — The bacteria's waste is acid, and acid has no way out of the jar. **(M1)**
- T5 — The rise is a population doubling toward a ceiling set by that finite sugar. **(M2)**
- T6 — The peak is the moment the sugar runs short, not a finish line. **(M2)**
- T7 — The fall is two failures at once: gas production slowing *and* the protein web being
  softened by the accumulated acid so it stops holding what is still being made. **(M2)**
- T8 — Temperature multiplies the speed of all of it, so a clock-based feeding schedule is a
  bet on the room. **(M2)**
- T9 — The acid ratchets up across days, and below about pH 3.5 the yeast stalls while the
  bacteria carry on — the culture goes sour, sluggish, and thin. **(M3)**
- T10 — Feeding is mostly *dilution*: the feed ratio decides what fraction of yesterday's acid
  survives into today's jar. **(M3)**
- T11 — Starvation, cold and acid **stall**; heat above about 40 °C, drying out, and mould
  **kill**. Hooch is a stall signal, not a death certificate. **(M3)**
- T12 — Therefore: their two starters were almost certainly never dead, and the third one
  lives on a bigger feed at a stable temperature. **(M3)**

This needs the whole course. One paragraph could say "it over-fermented," which is a label,
not an answer, and would leave them making the same feed.

The throughline goes *further into the mechanism they asked about* — rise and death. It does
not slide sideways into bread: no bulk fermentation, no shaping, no scoring, no crumb. Those
are a different course and they are the tempting detour here.

### Modules

**Module 1 — "The crowd in the jar."** What is alive in there, what it eats, and what the two
tenants leave behind. Four sections.

- M1-C1 — The jar holds two different kinds of living thing: wild yeast (a single-celled
  fungus, the same family as bread yeast) and lactic acid bacteria (the same group that turns
  milk into yogurt). Roughly ten to a hundred bacteria for every yeast cell.
- M1-C2 — Flour is not food yet. Starch is a long chain of sugar units locked end to end.
  Flour carries its own enzymes — proteins that cut things apart — that snip the chain into
  small sugars. Whole wheat carries more of those enzymes and more microbes on the bran, which
  is why a spoon of it wakes a jar up.
- M1-C3 — Yeast waste is carbon dioxide gas plus a little alcohol. The gas is caught in
  gluten, the stretchy web wheat protein forms when flour meets water. The rise is trapped
  gas. The jar does not gain material; on a scale it loses a little.
- M1-C4 — Bacteria waste is acid: lactic acid, the mild yogurt one, and acetic acid, the sharp
  vinegar one. The gas leaves through the lid. The acid stays.
- One sentence on the trade, not more: the bacteria eat a sugar the yeast largely cannot, and
  leak out one it can, which is why these two coexist instead of one crowding the other out.

**Module 2 — "The rise, the peak, and the long slide down."** The shape of one feed, in time.
Four sections. Arc: mechanism → the leap → the mess → the reveal.

- M2-C1 — The rise is a crowd doubling toward a ceiling, not a pile getting bigger. Doubling
  time, not amount added, sets the pace. *(rests on M1-C1, M1-C3)*
- M2-C2 — The peak is the moment the sugar runs short. It is a shortage, not a finish line,
  and where it lands is set almost entirely by how much old starter you kept back. *(rests on
  M1-C2, M2-C1)*
- M2-C3 — The fall is two failures at the same time: gas made per hour drops as sugar runs
  out, and the accumulated acid softens the gluten web so a shrinking fraction of the gas is
  held. Gas is still being produced at hour twelve. *(rests on M1-C3, M1-C4, M2-C2)*
- M2-C4 — Every rate here roughly doubles for each 10 °C the jar warms. A feeding schedule set
  by the clock is a bet on the room temperature. *(rests on M2-C1, M2-C2, M2-C3)*

**Module 3 — "Why it dies, and how the third one lives."** Five sections.

- M3-C1 — Acid accumulates and never leaves. A fresh mix sits near pH 6; a ripe jar near pH 4;
  a long-overdue one near pH 3.5. pH is the sourness number: lower is more sour. *(rests on M1-C4)*
- M3-C2 — Below about pH 3.5 the yeast slows hard while the bacteria keep going, so the
  population tilts. The result is a jar that smells strong and lifts nothing. *(rests on M3-C1,
  M2-C3)*
- M3-C3 — Feeding is mostly dilution. A 1:1:1 feed leaves a third of yesterday's acid in the
  jar; a 1:5:5 feed leaves a eleventh. Under-feeding ratchets the acid up day over day.
  *(rests on M3-C1, M2-C2)* — **this is where their half-cup-and-half-cup routine gets named.**
- M3-C4 — What actually kills, and what only stalls. Kills: above roughly 40 °C (104 °F, and
  hot tap water is hotter than that), drying to a crust, mould with fuzz or colour. Stalls:
  cold, starvation, acid. Hooch is a stall signal. *(rests on M3-C2)*
- M3-C5 — The protocol, written for their kitchen and their measuring cups.

### Gradable checks — every item, with the misconception each wrong option encodes

**Module 1, gate after §2 (tests §1):**
*Your jar smells like yogurt but has not risen. Which tenant is definitely working?*
- The bacteria — **correct.**
- The yeast — *misconception: smell and gas come from the same organism.*
- Both equally — *misconception: the two populations are one undifferentiated "starter."*
- Neither; smell means it is spoiling — *misconception: sour smell = spoilage.*

**Module 1, gate after §3 (tests §2):**
*Order these: water wets flour · enzymes cut starch into sugar · a tenant absorbs the sugar ·
gas is released.* Correct order as listed. The common wrong order puts absorption before
cutting — *misconception: flour is already food.*

**Module 1, instrument check (the flour dial):**
*Set the dial to 100% white and then to 25% whole wheat. Which readout moves more — sugar
released in the first three hours, or time to first bubbles?* Both move; sugar released moves
first and drives the other. Distractors encode *misconception: whole wheat helps because it
"has more nutrients," rather than because it carries more of the enzyme that makes sugar.*

**Module 1, end-of-module set (4 items):**
1. MCQ — *Jar A smells sharply of vinegar, jar B of plain yogurt. What differs?* Distractors
   encode: *sharper smell = more yeast*; *sharper smell = spoiled*; *smell has nothing to do
   with the tenants.* Correct: a different mix of the two acids the bacteria make.
2. Numeric with tolerance — *A jar weighs 480 g at feeding and doubles in volume. Weight at
   peak, ±5 g?* 480 (a gram or two under). Targets *rise = added material.*
3. Drag-to-label — label a schematic of one bubble in the paste: gluten web · trapped carbon
   dioxide · yeast cell · bacterium · dissolved acid. Mislabelling gas as "air" targets
   *misconception: the bubbles are air stirred in.*
4. MCQ — *You feed with flour that has been in the cupboard four years. What is most reduced?*
   Correct: enzyme activity, so sugar is released more slowly. Distractors encode *flour goes
   "stale" and loses calories*; *old flour has no gluten*; *nothing changes, flour is flour.*

## BOUNDARY TURN

You got the sequencing right: B is further along than A, and being further along is why it
smells the way it does. That is the correct read, and it is the part most people miss.

Two things in it need fixing, and they are the same mistake wearing two hats.

**The sour is not the yeast.** The sour is the bacteria — the second tenant from module 1, the
yogurt-makers. They make acid; they do not make gas. So a sharp smell tells you the bacteria
have been busy, and it tells you nothing directly about the yeast. In jar B the bacteria are
thriving. That is what the smell is evidence of, and it is the only thing it is evidence of.

**The flat top is not a death certificate either.** Flat means the jar is not *holding* gas
right now. It does not mean gas has stopped being made. Those come apart, and the gap between
them is most of what your 2 pm jar was doing by 11 pm. Module 2 is built on that gap.

Now the flour question, because it is the useful one and the answer is not what you would
guess: **adding more flour is the smallest part of what feeding does.** When you throw out most
of the jar and stir in fresh flour and water, the food is the visible half. The half that
actually rescues a sluggish jar is that you have removed most of what was already dissolved in
there — and that is the part your half-cup-of-starter-plus-half-cup-of-flour routine barely
touches. That is module 3, and I am not going to hand you the punchline early, because it lands
much harder once you can see the shape of a single feed. But hold on to the fact that your
routine is the specific thing being examined when we get there.

One from module 1 first, in your own words, no looking back: **flour goes in the jar dry.
Something has to happen to it before any tenant can eat it. What?**
